Quick answer. Anthropic plans to price its IPO on 18 November and start trading on Nasdaq as ANT on 19 November, at $70 to $78 a share. Nothing announced so far changes Claude plans or prices; what changes is that its costs, margins and choices get reported in public every quarter.

What happened

Anthropic, the company behind Claude, is about to become a listed company. In an updated filing on 10 November it set a range of $70 to $78 per share for 825 million Class A shares, to trade on the Nasdaq Global Select Market under the symbol ANT. Its own IPO page says the roadshow started on 10 November, the final price is expected on 18 November, and trading should begin on 19 November.

The money involved is large. At the top of the range the public sale would bring in up to $64.35 billion, CNBC reported. Separately, seven partners (AMD, Broadcom, Google, Micron, Microsoft, Nvidia and TSMC) agreed to buy $26 billion of non-voting Class N stock at the IPO price, according to TechCrunch.

Reports give two valuation figures. CNBC and TechCrunch put the top of the range at about $1.4 trillion on a fully diluted basis. Fox Business put the target at up to $1.02 trillion. Either way it is below the $2 trillion that investors had talked about earlier this year, and above the $965 billion valuation from Anthropic's May funding round.

Retail buyers get an unusually big slice. People familiar with the deal told CNBC that Anthropic is aiming for about 20% retail allocation, against 5% to 10% in a typical IPO. The IPO page says your own broker or investing app decides whether you are eligible to apply.

Why does it matter to people who use Claude?

Most readers here will never buy a share, but plenty pay for Claude or run tools built on it. The prospectus gives the most detailed public view yet of the business behind the product.

The growth is real. Anthropic reported $17.0 billion of revenue in the third quarter and more than 11,900 customers paying at least $100,000 a year. Nine-month revenue was $33.24 billion, up roughly 1,200% on the same period last year, per CNBC. By the end of October its run-rate revenue had reached about $81 billion, roughly $10 billion more than a month earlier, TechCrunch reported.

The costs are just as real. As of September, Anthropic had committed to future spending of roughly $900 billion, mostly on computing infrastructure, including deals with Google, Amazon, Microsoft and SpaceX. About $70 billion of that falls due in the next year, and roughly half by the end of 2030, CNBC reported.

That is the link to what you pay. Anthropic's roadshow pitch, as CNBC described it, has compute eating 45% to 50% of revenue in the long run, with an adjusted operating margin near 35% as the goal. In the third quarter, the IPO page shows a 15% adjusted operating margin and a GAAP operating margin of minus 3%. Closing that gap means revenue has to keep growing faster than compute bills. Price is one lever for that. Usage, efficiency and selling more seats are others.

The profit picture, read carefully

Anthropic reported a nine-month net loss of $104 billion, but CNBC says most of it came from changes in the fair value of convertible notes and derivative liabilities, a non-cash accounting charge. On a GAAP basis the operating loss was $1.26 billion through September. On Anthropic's adjusted basis, which leaves out stock-based pay and some other items, operating income was $4.08 billion. Fox Business noted operating cash flow was positive at $6.6 billion.

Analysts split on it. A City Index analyst called the offering "fairly bullish," per Fox Business. A Swissquote Bank analyst told Reuters, as quoted by Fox, that investors are now looking hard at what that growth costs, and CNBC's Jim Cramer framed buying the stock as a bet that growth outruns the spending.

Who controls the company after listing?

Anthropic is a Delaware public benefit corporation. The filing says it must balance shareholders' financial interests, the interests of people affected by its conduct, and its stated purpose of developing advanced AI responsibly. The founders hold a Class F share with 50.1% of the voting power, and a Long-Term Benefit Trust elects a board majority, so ordinary Class A buyers will have limited influence, TechCrunch reported.

What we don't know yet

The final price is not set. People familiar with the deal told CNBC the book was already covered across the $70 to $78 range, and markets can still move before 18 November.

Neither the filing nor the coverage of it points to a change in Claude plan prices. Where it mentions the Claude Pro, Max and Ultra subscriptions, it is to explain how a revenue metric is calculated. Any claim that the IPO will make Claude cheaper or dearer is a guess, not a fact from the prospectus.

The safety section also has a fresh warning. Fortune reported that Anthropic's internal estimates suggest future models could soon reach a threshold in its own Responsible Scaling Policy tied to AI speeding up AI research. The company says no current model has crossed it and that it is weighing policy changes to keep humans in charge.

What to watch

  • 18 and 19 November. The final price, and how the stock trades in its first days. A weak debut could add pressure to cut costs.
  • The first earnings report. Watch the adjusted operating margin and the share of revenue going to compute. Those two numbers say more about future pricing than any press release.
  • Founder share sales. Each of the seven co-founders plans to sell nearly 3.4 million shares in the offering, TechCrunch reported. Watch how insiders behave once lock-ups end.

If your team pays for Claude through the API, now is a good time to measure what you actually use. A token counter helps you see where the spend goes before any price change arrives.

My take

For a business already on Claude, I'd change nothing this week. The filing shows a company with fast growth, heavy compute commitments and a governance setup that keeps control with the founders and the trust, which makes a sudden swing in product direction less likely than at a typical listed tech firm. What I would do is treat AI spend like any other vendor cost: know your monthly usage, keep a second model tested for your key workflows, and read the first two quarterly reports for margin and compute numbers. If the margin gap is closing through efficiency, prices probably hold. If it isn't, I'd expect the pressure to show up in plan limits or pricing tiers first, and I'd want a fallback ready before that happens.

Frequently asked questions

When does the Anthropic IPO start trading?

Anthropic expects to set the final share price on 18 November 2026 and to start trading on the Nasdaq Global Select Market on 19 November 2026 (US Eastern time), under the ticker symbol ANT. The company says these dates are subject to regulatory clearance, so they can still move.

What is the Anthropic IPO price range?

Anthropic set a range of $70 to $78 per share for 825 million Class A shares. At the top of the range the public sale raises up to $64.35 billion. Seven partners, including Google and Nvidia, also agreed to buy $26 billion of non-voting stock at the final IPO price.

Will Claude prices change after the Anthropic IPO?

The prospectus does not announce any change to Claude plan or API prices. It does show heavy compute commitments and a long-term margin target of about 35%, so pricing will depend on whether revenue keeps growing faster than compute costs. Any claim of a price change today is a guess.

Can investors in India apply for Anthropic IPO shares?

Anthropic's IPO page says investors request shares through a participating brokerage platform, and that the broker or investing app confirms whether you are eligible. Indian investors should check with their own platform. Shares bought after listing are bought at the market price at that time, which is a separate route from the IPO allocation.

Who controls Anthropic after it goes public?

Anthropic is a public benefit corporation. Its founders hold a Class F share with 50.1% of the voting power, and the Long-Term Benefit Trust elects a majority of the board, so buyers of Class A shares have limited influence over how the company is run, TechCrunch reported.
