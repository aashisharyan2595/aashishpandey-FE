Quick answer. 1Password says an attacker hijacked an employee's corporate GitHub account and downloaded names, emails and account metadata for some customers. The company says vaults and the keys guarding them were not touched, so phishing is the main risk it flags.

What happened

1Password put out a breach update on November 25, 2026. Its team started looking into the problem on November 19, when they spotted that someone had got into a staff member's work GitHub login. By the company's own account, that login let the attacker read code, slip bad code into backend services, and pull customer admin records out through it.

What went out, and what stayed put

The affected records belong to some customers, not all. They hold names, sign-in emails, account IDs, when an account was created and last used, public keys, and Secure Remote Password settings. 1Password says the public keys are meant to be public and the SRP settings can't open an account.

Some emailed invitation, provisioning and recovery tokens were in there too. On their own they don't unlock anything, the company says, because a second step is needed: an SSO sign-in or a code sent to your inbox. A small number of people mid-invite or mid-recovery got a direct heads-up, and the code is now ten letters and numbers instead of six digits.

The attacker also copied private source code and reached the CI/CD system that the company relies on to ship its product. 1Password says that system stores infrastructure secrets, so it rotated every credential that might have been exposed.

Vaults and what's in them, the keys guarding them, recovery keys, and the apps and extensions were not hit, it says. It doesn't keep your account password or Secret Key at all.

Why phishing is the real risk

A real name, a real email and a last sign-in time make a fake message look believable. SQ Magazine points out that this is the sort of data scammers reuse after a breach. 1Password tells customers to treat any surprise email, text or call about 1Password, an invite or account recovery as suspect, and says it will never ask for your password or Secret Key.

For a small team: warn staff today, question any pending invite nobody can explain, and never read an emailed code to a caller.

What we don't know yet

Plenty is missing. No customer count has been given, and MLQ reports that 1Password didn't answer TechCrunch's questions about it. We also don't know how the GitHub account was taken over or how long the access lasted, a gap Startup Fortune also flags. The company reports no signs of takeovers using the tokens, which speaks only for its own systems and those tokens.

What to watch

Outside security experts are checking the cleanup, and 1Password says it will revise the post if anything changes. A customer count and a cause are what to look for.

My take

On the company's account, the part that matters held: vault contents sit behind keys 1Password doesn't have. I'd still treat the coming week as phishing season for anyone with an account, and I'd want to know how one GitHub login reached production before calling this closed.

Frequently asked questions

Were my 1Password passwords stolen?

1Password says no. By its account, vault contents were never reached, the keys guarding vaults are untouched, and it holds neither your account password nor your Secret Key. What the attacker pulled was account admin data for some users: names, sign-in emails, identifiers and activity times.

Do I need to change my 1Password master password?

The company says nothing needs changing on your account. Its advice is to stay alert for scams instead. Be suspicious of any unexpected message about 1Password, an invitation or account recovery, and type your credentials only into the official app or site that you opened yourself.

How did the attacker get in?

1Password says someone took over an employee's corporate GitHub account, which it began investigating on November 19, and used it to reach code and push malicious changes to backend services. It has not explained how the account was compromised. The account is now disabled and exposed credentials were replaced.

What are the emailed tokens that were taken?

They come from emails about account invitations, provisioning and recovery. 1Password says a token alone can't open an account, since a second step is required: SSO sign-in or a code sent to the registered email. That code is now ten characters long, and the few people affected were contacted.
