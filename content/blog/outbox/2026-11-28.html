Quick answer. Attackers are exploiting CVE-2026-76466, a 9.8-rated flaw in clustered Cisco Expressway gateways that gives root control with no login. The first fixed release for X15.5.3 and earlier is X15.5.20, and Cisco says there is no workaround.

What happened?

Cisco Expressway is a gateway that lets remote users reach voice, video and messaging tools from outside the office network. The Register describes its jobs as firewall traversal, remote access without a VPN, and call routing.

Cisco disclosed CVE-2026-76466 on October 21 and shipped a fix the same day, saying it knew of no abuse. That changed in November. On Thursday, November 19, Russian security firm F6 said it was investigating what it called the first observed case of the bug being exploited, The Register reports, shortly after a public proof-of-concept exploit appeared. A day later, the US cyber agency CISA put the flaw in its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog with a due date of November 23 for federal agencies.

The bug scores 9.8 out of 10 and needs no login. Cisco says an attacker who holds certain persistent cookies can reach the database service and could then "execute arbitrary commands as root on the underlying operating system of an affected device."

Why does it matter?

Root means full control of the box, and the Expressway-E units sit on the network edge by design. In the case F6 described, attackers broke into two internet-facing Expressway devices, used one to reach two more cluster nodes on the internal network, and then started preparing tools to move further, per Infosecurity Magazine.

Those tools were custom Python scripts built to scan the network, find shared SMB folders, talk to SQL Server and WinRM, and search GitLab repositories for credentials. F6 said the scripts suggested the attacker used an AI assistant, according to The Register.

Who is exposed?

Both Expressway-C and Expressway-E, virtual or physical, but only when the release is vulnerable and the devices are set up in a cluster, according to The Register. Cisco's explanation is two-part: persistent cookies, plus client certificates that aren't validated when TLS Verify Mode is enabled for clustering.

On versions, a reading of Cisco's advisory by BrainDetox gives these ranges. If you run X15.5.3 or earlier, the first fixed release is X15.5.20. If you run X14 or earlier, Cisco says to migrate to a fixed release. The same write-up says Cisco's table has no row for X15.5.4 through X15.5.19, so it can't tell you whether those are affected.

Don't take comfort from not running a cluster. BrainDetox notes that Cisco published a separate Expressway hardening advisory the same day, rated High at 8.6, which applies whatever your configuration. It calls that conclusion its own interpretation, and it's a sensible one.

3 steps to take now

  1. List every Expressway you run. Note whether each is Expressway-C or Expressway-E, its release, and whether it's in a cluster. Edge units facing the internet come first.
  2. Upgrade to X15.5.20 or later. Cisco says there's no workaround apart from updating. If you have no service contract, BrainDetox says to contact Cisco's Technical Assistance Center with your serial number and the advisory URL.
  3. Look for signs someone's already in. F6 says one indicator is a "wombat@127.0.0.1" nodeup entry in the Erlang system log (erlang.log). If you can't patch today, F6 advises restricting TCP ports 4371 to 4372 at the firewall so only your trusted cluster nodes can connect.

What don't we know yet?

Cisco's own position is slightly different from everyone else's. The Register says Cisco updated its advisory on Monday to acknowledge the CISA listing while noting it had no direct evidence of active exploitation. Cisco also said it "continues to strongly recommend that customers upgrade to a fixed software release," per Cyber Daily. So the proof of attacks comes from F6 and CISA, not from Cisco.

F6 described one organization. Nobody has said how many others are hit. CISA's catalog entry lists use in ransomware campaigns as "Unknown" and marks forensic triage as required for agencies. SecNews adds that F6 tied the attack to a proof-of-concept published on GitHub, which is how quickly attackers can move once a working exploit is public.

No mainstream news outlets had covered this when I wrote it, so the sources are CISA's own catalog entry and trade sites that quote Cisco's advisory.

What to watch

Watch for a revision to Cisco's advisory, more incident reports from F6 or other responders, and any change to CISA's ransomware field.

My take

I'd treat this as a same-day job, not a next-sprint one. The fix has been out since October, the exploit is public, and CISA gave federal agencies until November 23, a date that's already behind us. If an Expressway of yours was exposed and unpatched during the last few weeks, I'd patch and then check for intruders, because an upgrade doesn't remove someone who's already inside. For a small business that gets its Expressway through an IT partner, the question to send today is simple: which release are we on, and are we clustered?

Frequently asked questions

What is CVE-2026-76466?

It's a critical flaw, rated 9.8 out of 10, in Cisco Expressway gateways. In clustered setups, an attacker with the right persistent cookies can reach the database service and run commands as root without logging in. Cisco disclosed it on October 21 and released a fix. CISA added it to its exploited-vulnerabilities list on November 20.

Which Cisco Expressway versions are fixed?

For X15.5.3 and earlier, the first fixed release is X15.5.20. For X14 and earlier, Cisco says to migrate to a fixed release. A third-party reading of Cisco's table says it has no row for X15.5.4 through X15.5.19, so check Cisco's advisory directly for those versions.

Is there a workaround for the Cisco Expressway flaw?

Cisco says no workaround exists apart from upgrading to a fixed release. If you can't upgrade today, security firm F6 suggests limiting TCP ports 4371 to 4372 at the firewall so that only your trusted cluster nodes can connect. Treat that as a stopgap while you schedule the upgrade, not a fix.

How do I know if my Expressway was attacked?

F6 names one indicator: a nodeup entry for wombat@127.0.0.1 in the Erlang system log, erlang.log, on the device. Absence of that entry doesn't prove you're clean. CISA says federal agencies must do forensic triage, so if the device was exposed and unpatched, ask your IT partner to review it properly.

Do I need to worry if I don't use clusters?

Cisco's flaw description applies to clustered devices. But Cisco also published a separate Expressway hardening advisory, rated High, that applies regardless of configuration. A write-up of the advisories concludes unclustered devices should upgrade to the same fixed release too. That's their interpretation, so confirm with Cisco or your reseller.
