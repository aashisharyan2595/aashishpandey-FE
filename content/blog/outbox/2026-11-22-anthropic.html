Quick answer. Anthropic priced its IPO at $83, opened at $111.50 on 19 November and closed its first day at $97.17, 17% above the offer price, on a day when the broader market fell more than 2%. Supporters point to growth and cash; critics point to about $900 billion in spending commitments.

What happened on Nasdaq?

Anthropic priced its IPO on 18 November at $83 a share. That was above the $70 to $78 range, per TechCrunch. The offering was 980,000,000 Class A shares, and the stock started trading on Nasdaq the next day under the ticker ANT. It opened at $111.50, about 34% above the offer price.

That didn't last. By the close, per NBC News, ANT had slipped to $97.17, still 17% above $83. The S&P 500, the Nasdaq and the Dow all closed more than 2% lower, so the IPO arrived on a bad day for the whole market. NBC also reported that after-hours trading had the shares down more than 2.5%.

On 20 November Anthropic said the sale had closed. With the underwriters' extra shares included, it sold 1,127,000,000 shares for about $93.5 billion, of which about $91.6 billion went to the company. A separate private placement of Class N shares to AMD, Broadcom, Google, Micron, Microsoft, Nvidia and TSMC Partners added about $26.0 billion. Total gross proceeds came to about $119.5 billion. TechCrunch calls it the largest IPO ever by money raised, ahead of SpaceX's $75 billion.

What does the valuation look like?

At the IPO price, TechCrunch reports a fully diluted valuation of roughly $1.5 trillion. That is up from $965 billion in the private round in May, and below the $2 trillion the company was reportedly aiming for earlier. NBC put the company at about $1.76 trillion after the first close, counting restricted shares and stock options.

The prospectus numbers, as Fox Business summarised them: $33.2 billion in revenue for the first nine months of 2026, up 1,206% on the same period a year earlier. A net loss of $104 billion, which Fox says was largely driven by non-cash accounting charges. Against that, the company generated $6.6 billion of operating cash.

Why are people still arguing about it?

Because the same filing carries the bill. Anthropic disclosed about $900.9 billion in spending commitments, mostly compute and infrastructure, running through 2045. About $70.7 billion of that is due within 12 months of 30 September, according to Fox Business.

Reuters columnist Mike Dolan, in a column carried by Devdiscourse, cites the credit rating firm Egan-Jones. It flags those commitments as close to four times Anthropic's combined cash, trailing revenue and share sale proceeds at the time, and notes that about 44% of revenue came through cloud marketplaces run by Amazon, Google and Microsoft. It also points out that many top customers pay by consumption and can cut spending when they like. Its report concluded: "There are still massive questions left unanswered about Anthropic."

The other side has numbers too. TechCrunch quotes SemiAnalysis's Joey Brookhart estimating Anthropic needs about $280 billion in cumulative revenue over four years to pay for its compute, which he calls achievable. Gil Luria of D.A. Davidson told the same outlet the company will hold more cash than any company ever has. TechCrunch puts the cash pile at about $220 billion.

Then there is the circularity question. Amazon holds about 14.7% and Google about 13.7%, and both are also major suppliers. TechCrunch describes how that money can pass from investor to supplier and back. Its argument is that a public listing changes this, because future funding comes from public investors and bonds rather than suppliers.

An earlier sceptic is worth a mention. In October the research firm New Constructs said Anthropic could be worth as little as $144 billion. That was its lowest scenario, built from reports of the leaked financials because the firm had not seen the prospectus. Its record is mixed: WeWork pulled its IPO after a similar label in 2019, while DoorDash held up. Anthropic did not respond to CNBC for that report, according to The Next Web.

What don't we know yet?

Plenty. One day of trading says little about where the price settles. Fox Business reports that up to 315 million shares held by employees and other non-founder holders could become eligible for sale on day one, generally up to 10% of their locked-up holdings, and NBC reported that early signs pointed to those sales adding to the heavy volume. Other holders face staggered lock-ups of up to a year.

We also don't know how the next quarterly report will land. Dolan argues that daily trading and quarterly reporting may push Anthropic toward more transparency on token use and profitability, so the next report matters.

What about Dario Amodei's speech?

Before the opening bell, Dario Amodei said Anthropic would not bend to business or political pressure. TechCrunch quotes him: "we will pace the training and deployment of AI systems, taking as long as is necessary to have confidence that they are safe and will remain under human control." Whether that squeezes against public-market pressure for growth is something the next few quarters will show.

Control is built into the share structure. Per Fox Business, founders keep 50.1% of the voting power through a special class of shares, and the Long-Term Benefit Trust generally picks a majority of the board.

What can you do about it?

If you use Claude at work, nothing in the coverage I read says plans or prices are changing. If you are an investor, that is a separate decision and this post isn't advice. For readers in India, Goodreturns explains that you can't apply through domestic IPO platforms, and that resident individuals can buy US-listed shares through an overseas brokerage account under the RBI's Liberalised Remittance Scheme, which allows up to $250,000 per financial year. Check the current rules and the tax treatment with your broker first.

My take: the first-day move looks like a fair summary of the whole debate. Real demand, with a price that gave back about 13% between the open and the close as the wider market fell. I'd hold the headline valuation loosely for now and watch the first quarterly report and the lock-up dates.
